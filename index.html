About

I am a Ph.D. student at The Pennsylvania State University, pursuing a dual-title degree through the Department of Political Science and the Center for Social Data Analytics (SoDA).

My research focuses on two questions: how authoritarian rulers respond to mass mobilization, and why some citizens in post-authoritarian democracies, such as South Korea, valorize former dictators as “guardians of liberal democracy.”

Prior to Penn State, I completed a master’s degree in Political Science and earned a bachelor’s degree in Economics at Korea University.

Publications

Lee, Juhyeok. Forthcoming. “Elections and State Concessions to Anti-government Mobilisation in Authoritarian Regimes.” Government and Opposition.

Abstract

How do elections shape authoritarian rulers' responses to anti-government mobilisation? Building on theories of state responses to contention, this article argues that electoral timing can increase the political consequences of mobilisations and the strategic returns to accommodation, particularly where organised partisan alternatives are available. Using the Covariate Balancing Propensity Score method on protest data from 68 authoritarian countries between 2003 and 2020, the analysis shows that dictators are more inclined to grant concessions as anti-government mobilisation occurs closer to electoral boundaries, and that the pre-election relationship is stronger in regimes that permit multiparty competition. Moreover, this positive temporal variation is concentrated in regimes with competitive elections; regimes with non-competitive elections exhibit no comparable pattern across the electoral calendar. The findings show how electoral pressures shape concessionary responses to anti-government mobilisation and clarify the temporal and institutional structures of authoritarian responsiveness.

Lee, Juhyeok, and Nam Kyu Kim. 2026. “Party Organizational Strength and Voter Turnout in Authoritarian Regimes.” Electoral Studies 99: 103034.

DOI
Abstract

This study examines how ruling party organizational strength influences voter turnout in authoritarian elections. While parties and elections serve as crucial instruments for authoritarian survival, their effect on voter mobilization remains understudied. We argue that organizationally robust ruling parties–characterized by extensive grassroots networks and deep societal ties–enhance regimes’ capacity to manipulate electoral participation. However, dictators do not uniformly maximize turnout but selectively mobilize supporters while demobilizing opponents, particularly in competitive elections. Accordingly, we hypothesize that the effect of party strength on turnout is conditional on electoral competitiveness. Analyzing 94 autocracies from 1970–2019, we find that party organizational strength significantly increases turnout in non-competitive elections but has negligible effects in competitive contests. This pattern intensified after the Cold War as competitive authoritarianism proliferated globally.

Partisan Criticism, Judicial Consensus, and Public Support for Constitutional Court

Lee, Juhyeok, Siuk Lee, Nam Kyu Kim.

Abstract

Courts in polarized democracies are routinely attacked by the parties that lose before them. Public support is often described as a court’s main shield against such attacks, but existing theories disagree about what a partisan attack does to that shield. We ask three questions. Does partisan criticism lower support for the court among the critic’s supporters or raise it among the critic’s opponents? Does it change evaluations of the ruling more than institutional loyalty? Does a divided bench make the court look more political and change the effect of partisan criticism? We answer them with a preregistered 2 × 3 vignette survey experiment in South Korea (N = 1,200), where impeachment has drawn the Constitutional Court into partisan conflict. Respondents read that the Court dismissed a cabinet minister’s impeachment. The ruling was decided either unanimously or 5:4, and it was either uncriticized or attacked by one of the two major parties as defying the will of the people. Contrary to our preregistered expectation, we find that criticism from respondents’ own party did not lower diffuse support for the Court. Instead, out-partisan criticism triggered a defensive rally, increasing both diffuse and specific support. Criticism shifted evaluations of the ruling more than institutional loyalty. This suggests that partisan attacks polarize how citizens read rulings without draining the reservoir of support for the court. We also find that a divided bench made the ruling look more political but neither reduced legitimacy nor changed the effect of criticism. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that heightened perceptions of judicial politicization do not necessarily translate into diminished institutional legitimacy, highlighting the resilience of diffuse support even when the political neutrality of judicial decisions is called into question.

Party Origins, Institutions, and Populist Rhetoric in Authoritarian Regimes

Lee, Juhyeok.

Abstract

Conceptualizing populist rhetoric as a strategy of authoritarian legitimation, this study documents variation in its use and explains how party origins and institutional arrangements shape that variation. Populist rhetoric can help authoritarian rulers mobilize popular support, deflect blame, and reinforce existing sources of legitimacy, but it may also entail costs of establishing credible claims and risks of antagonizing regime elites and provoking public backlash. I argue that authoritarian ruling parties originating in revolutionary or independence movements use more populist rhetoric and that multiparty elections and legislative power-sharing moderate this relationship. Analyzing 97 parties in 72 countries between 1970 and 2019, I find that movement-origin parties employ more populist rhetoric, and this gap narrows as multiparty electoral alternatives emerge and legislative power-sharing increases. This study advances populism research by documenting systematic variation in populist rhetoric within authoritarian regimes and explaining its organizational and institutional sources. It also contributes to research on authoritarian legitimation by explaining how movement origins influence subsequent ruling parties’ claims to justifying their rule, and how institutional arrangements alter the costs and benefits of using these appeals.

The Transition of Authoritarian Ruling Parties and Women's Cabinet Representation after Democratization

Lee, Juhyeok.

Abstract

While democracy is widely associated with advancements in women's political empowerment, significant variation in female cabinet representation persists across democratic regimes. This study investigates how the trajectories of authoritarian ruling parties after democratization influence women's inclusion in executive office. Specifically, it argues that authoritarian legacies, particularly the strategies adopted by former ruling parties, shape both the structural and political conditions that affect women's cabinet representation. The findings show that democracies where authoritarian ruling parties exited power and reinvented themselves as pro-democratic actors tend to exhibit significantly higher levels of female cabinet representation. This effect emerges approximately four years after democratization and grows over time, with a nine-percentage-point gap observed by the tenth year. These results highlight the importance of post-authoritarian party transitions in shaping gender-inclusive political development, suggesting that meaningful progress toward gender parity in executive leadership requires not only democratic institutions, but also a decisive break from authoritarian legacies.
